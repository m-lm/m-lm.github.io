This past spring I completed my senior capstone project with a team of peers. Some capstone projects are more contrived than others, but at my institution, the roster of assigned projects were virtually all hands-on and involved a real stakeholder such as a company or sponsor; in a way, it was like a mini-internship, which I appreciated. I also had the opportunity to work on a team for the first time in this setting. In the following sections, I'll reflect on some of the things I learned during the development process.

1. **Normalization of databases is not always necessary or even recommended.** In many introductory databases classes, we're taught to normalize our database schema to reduce redundancy and increase efficiency when storing and querying data. This is an important consideration whenever working with databases, but at this point I never really heard any reason for the opposite. It turns out that the number one reason for denormalizing your database is to optimize for read times. For example, if multiple tables are needed to normalize the data, then that also increases the number of expensive joins that are done even for common queries. This is especially a concern whenever your search engine is built directly on your database, as it was in our case. I was responsible for a Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) feature which incorporated both full-text and vector search. Our database of choice was PostgreSQL, and because we wanted simplicity for our particular situation, we opted for using Postgres’s built-in tsvector as well as the pgvector extension to allow us to perform text and vector search, respectively.  

    What I did not realize at the time was that my carefully designed 3NF schema was actually not very conducive for efficiently performing the search operations the system needed. I realized this was an inherent tradeoff that came with my decision of normalization which required workarounds (out of the scope of this post) if I did not want to return to the drawing board. In short, denormalizing the database introduced redundancy, but this was a feature--not a bug. My advisor (and databases professor) always said computer science is fundamentally about tradeoffs--case in point. But what I also take away for the future is to always ask deliberate questions about design choices and to not be afraid of revisiting them if they are critical.  

2. **Quality search is not necessarily equivalent to relevant search.** Since I was responsible for RAG and search, it was up to me to figure out how best to return the most relevant results. While I successfully designed and implemented the pipeline, I realized returning the top 10 retrieved results is really a drop in the bucket when compared to tens of thousands of possible results. Why those 10? If a user searched for a specific term or certain document, how do they know they got a good collection of results, and not just the most relevant for a very specific subset of the data? It doesn’t matter if I can surface 10 results that are 90%+ matches to my query, because what good is it if I get almost all identical (though relevant) results? What if what I'm looking for is in the next 10, or even 100? Especially for multi-hop retrieval and complex question answering, where coverage and comprehensiveness are paramount, this becomes a major problem.

    Often times, the last thing a user wants is not the same thing 10 times, it’s several very relevant results interleaved with tangential but related ones throughout. This is exactly what techniques like Maximal Marginal Relevance (MMR), a ranking algorithm that prevents redundancy in search by optimizing for both relevance to the query *and* distinction from already selected results, aim to achieve.  

    I view this as yet another example of where it is important to test your preconceived notions of what a user may want and what is considered “correct.” For many applications, using something like MMR to bolster results is more appropriate than simply sticking results into a cross-encoder reranker and hoping for the best. Up until this point, the projects I worked on would probably not be used in any serious manner by an actual user. This time around, things were slightly different. This experience helped me think more like a user, and not just a developer.  

3. **Scope is a guide, not a limit.** This is an important concept that I personally can have trouble with even in other aspects of life, but which is eminently applicable. From our initial and gathered requirements and use cases, it was our job to sift through and figure out the best way to implement them. Eventually, it would become clear which features would not make the cut. Rather than lament and overthink this eventuality, I quickly learned to accept it; otherwise, it will easily drag down the quality of your work in the more critical, time-laden parts of the project. I sometimes fall victim to my own perfectionism when instead it's better to build, evaluate, and reiterate, not letting perfect be the enemy of good. Because in reality, things aren’t always going to go perfectly despite tons of planning. Although I’m still green when it comes to complex, large-scale software development, I recognize how it would be debilitating to myself and my team if I were to try to account for absolutely everything. As the computer scientist Donald Knuth famously put it, “Premature optimization is the root of all evil.”  

Although I won’t go into the specifics of the project out of discretion, I know that the lessons I’ve learned from my capstone will be of great use in the future, and I hope to apply this same introspection to every project I work on moving forward.
